# Interview Playbook — synthesis of the corpus

Condensed from all 26 notes in `notes/`, arranged as the stages of an interview process.
Every claim is attributed. Authority marks follow the corpus convention:
**[F]** firsthand (was on the hiring side), **[P]** practitioner (went through it),
**[S]** secondhand (synthesizing others).

Where sources contradict each other, the contradiction is kept rather than averaged —
see [Stage 9](#stage-9--where-the-sources-disagree).

---

## Stage 0 — Application and resume

**The ATS does not reject you; a human never opened you.** Farah Sharghi **[F]** (ex-Google/
Uber/TikTok recruiter, screened 100,000+ resumes) argues the entire "beat the ATS" industry
is built on a metric third-party vendors invented. An ATS is a database — tracking, keyword
search that is functionally Ctrl+F, no scoring pass, no AI screen. Most platforms are 20–30-year-old
legacy systems built to track, not judge.
— [Beat the ATS](notes/beat-the-ats-they-lied-ex-google-recruiter-reveals-the.md)

- **The one thing that actually auto-rejects: knockout questions** on the application form
  (age, visa sponsorship), configured by the recruiter. This is the real mechanism behind
  "rejected in 30 seconds." **[F]**
- The "80% match score moves you to a human" figure comes from vendors selling resume
  audits (Jobscan named), not from any ATS. No one on the hiring team sees it. **[F]**
- White-font keyword stuffing backfires with a specific mechanism: the ATS extracts
  everything to plain text, so the hidden block appears in full when a recruiter copy-pastes
  your resume to the hiring manager. **[F]**
- Write for a human skimming in ~5 seconds; lead with quantified business impact, not duties. **[F]**
- **Under-titling is one of the most common resume mistakes** — people wait for the official
  promotion before claiming work they already do. Claiming the work you actually did is
  accurate representation, not inflation. Fix a title gap with a parenthetical:
  "Product Manager (functionally leading roadmap without formal title)." **[F]**
  — [Why "Lying" Gets You Hired](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-why-lying-gets-you-hired.md),
  [Beat the ATS](notes/beat-the-ats-they-lied-ex-google-recruiter-reveals-the.md)
- **The referral is the single strongest lever on an application.** Script: search the
  company's LinkedIn People tab for a mutual connection (same university, club, hometown),
  connect with a note naming the shared thing, ask for tips ahead of your interview. **[S]**
  — [6 Steps to ACE](notes/6-steps-to-ace-your-job-interview-pep-talk.md)

## Stage 1 — Prep

### How much

The corpus splits sharply. Emily Durham **[F]** (10 yrs HR/recruiting) caps prep at **one hour**
and names over-preparation as a disqualifier she saw repeatedly: a candidate visibly reading
from a script is "out of the race," because the format is supposed to read as conversation
([Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)). Erin McGoff **[S]**
caps _company research_ at 30–60 minutes ([6 Steps](notes/6-steps-to-ace-your-job-interview-pep-talk.md)).
Richard McMunn **[S]** goes the other way and tells you to bring written answers into the room
and read from them when stuck ([Under 10 Minutes](notes/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-under-10-minutes-last.md)).
Weight by authority: the two people who sat on the hiring side both warn against visible scripting.

### What to research

- **Not "what does the company do" but "why does this role exist — what is the business losing
  money or time on that this work is meant to fix?"** The role is a purchased remedy for a
  specific loss. Study earnings reports and stated goals, then connect your past work to them.
  Farah Sharghi **[F]** — [The Interview Secret](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-the-interview-secret-to.md)
- Four things to nail down: what the company does, its major competitors, why you want to work
  there, what skills the job requires. Emily Durham **[F]** —
  [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)
- Glassdoor's per-company **Interviews** tab for actual reported questions at that specific
  company. **[S]** — [6 Steps](notes/6-steps-to-ace-your-job-interview-pep-talk.md)
- Careers page for published benefits (401k match, parental leave) so you can raise them
  without asking cold. **[S]** — same
- LinkedIn profile-view notification is an asset, not a risk — it reads as proactive research. **[S]** — same

### Story inventory

- **Memorize examples, not answers.** List the skills the job description screens for, pre-stage
  one work/internship/school example per skill. Emily Durham **[F]** —
  [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)
- Behavioral interviews only test **five qualities**: leadership (really _initiative_), resilience,
  teamwork, influence/persuasion, and integrity under ethical conflict. The variety of questions
  is those five re-phrased. Matt Huang **[P]** (Google strategy, ex-BCG, ex-IB) —
  [Behavioral is shockingly uncomplicated](notes/answering-behavioral-interview-questions-is-shockingly.md)
- **Have at least two stories per quality** — a single story usually covers multiple qualities
  and gets consumed by an earlier question, then the follow-up catches you with nothing left. **[P]** — same
- Prepare against the **second- and third-order phrasing**, not the obvious one: "took the lead
  _even though you were not in a formal position of leadership_", "convince someone more senior
  _of an opinion they disagreed with you on_", "bring together a group _with disparate backgrounds_."
  The higher-order versions are where candidates freeze. **[P]** — same
- **Never rehearse from written-out paragraphs — rehearse from four or five bullets per story.**
  Mechanism: full text makes your brain optimize for recall, and the seams between remembered
  fragments are exactly where you sound scripted. Bullets force you to build the connective
  sentences live. **[P]** — same
- Mock interview dressed, without notes, with someone willing to be blunt — explicitly not a
  family member. **[S]** — [6 Steps](notes/6-steps-to-ace-your-job-interview-pep-talk.md)

### Dress and setup

- One level above what employees actually wear; err conservative. **[S]** —
  [6 Steps](notes/6-steps-to-ace-your-job-interview-pep-talk.md); one level more senior than the
  role itself, Emily Durham **[F]** — [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)
- Leila Hormozi **[F]** (founder/CEO who runs her own interviews) goes further: **overdressing has
  no downside in an interview specifically**, even dressing better than the CEO reads as impressive.
  — [Watch this BEFORE](notes/watch-this-before-your-job-interview.md)
- If ambiguous, just ask the recruiter. Farah Sharghi **[F]** —
  [The Interview Secret](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-the-interview-secret-to.md)
- Video: camera at eye level, face well lit, clean background, **wired headphones over Bluetooth**
  so audio can't drop. Sharghi's stated inference from a bad setup: _a candidate who can't manage
  a webcam is assumed to be unable to manage a project._ **[F]** — same
- 10 minutes early in person, 5 minutes early on a video link with mic/camera pre-checked. **[F]** — same
- Tidy the area the camera _doesn't_ show — cited basis is that a messy space degrades your own
  thinking during the call. **[S]** — [HBR Guide](notes/stand-out-in-a-job-interview-the-harvard-business-review.md)
- Have a hello-world file you can already run before a coding interview starts. **[F]** —
  [How to pass a coding interview with me](notes/how-to-pass-a-coding-interview-with-me-robert-heaton.md)

## Stage 2 — The first 30 seconds

- Cited research claim: people form opinions about your personality and intelligence within the
  **first 30 seconds** — the stated justification for rehearsing the opening rather than the
  answers. **[S]** — [HBR Guide](notes/stand-out-in-a-job-interview-the-harvard-business-review.md)
- Hormozi's **[F]** firsthand account: a candidate she otherwise liked didn't smile for the first
  20–30 minutes and seemed down. It cost her the whole second half of that interview _plus an
  additional interview_ to talk herself back into the candidate. Her model: the hiring manager
  decides subconsciously in the first few minutes and then spends the rest being argued out of
  or into it. — [Watch this BEFORE](notes/watch-this-before-your-job-interview.md)
- Mechanical fix for video: **start smiling before your camera turns on**, so the first frame is
  already the expression you want. **[F]** — same

### "How are you?" is scored

- Farah Sharghi **[F]**: she is silently asking _"is this how they're going to show up on a hard
  day at work?"_ Always answer positively. Never open with a cold, a rough week, or traffic.
  — [Why "Lying" Gets You Hired](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-why-lying-gets-you-hired.md)
- Emily Durham **[F]**: give a real, _specific_ answer so the recruiter can react to it — it's the
  only reliably available rapport slot before the formal questions start.
  — [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)

### What character you're playing

Not "someone trying hard to get this job" but "someone who wants a great conversation with the
person across from you." Paired with the improv instruction to treat every conversational opening
as "yes, and" rather than a cue to deploy a prepared block. Cathy Salit via HBR **[S]** —
[HBR Guide](notes/stand-out-in-a-job-interview-the-harvard-business-review.md)

## Stage 3 — "Tell me about yourself"

Five sources give five different structures. They agree on almost nothing but the stakes, so
here they are side by side.

| Source                                    | Shape                                                                                           | Length                        |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| The Companies Expert **[F]** (former CEO) | "As you can see from my resume…" → **oldest to newest** → fixed closing transition              | 45–60 s                       |
| InterviewGold **[S]**                     | **ICAN(+)**: Introduction, Career+achievements, Attributes/skills, Next, optional Personal      | 1–2 min entry, 2–3 min senior |
| Emily Durham **[F]**                      | who you are, what you do, what you want — with a metric and an explicit callback to the posting | under 90 s–2 min              |
| Cass Thompson **[S]**                     | professional only, one detail per past role tied to this job, close on why you're here today    | 1–3 min                       |
| Vinh Giang **[S]**                        | a personal **origin story** linked to the role                                                  | ~3 min                        |

**The strongest single argument in this section** — The Companies Expert **[F]**: because they read
your resume and _still_ invited you in, everything on it is **pre-cleared content** — none of it
triggered elimination. Restating it carries near-zero risk; volunteering anything new introduces
material that has never been screened. He frames recruiting as an elimination game with **hidden
criteria** the candidate never sees, and gives a real one: a hiring manager eliminating a candidate
who volunteered support for a rival sports team, rationalized as fit.
— [Tell Me About Yourself](notes/tell-me-about-yourself-best-answer-from-former-ceo.md)

Other durable points:

- Mirror the employer's own language from the job description and cover its top three skills.
  Stated reason is mechanical: it makes it easy for the panel **to score you** against a sheet
  keyed to the posting. **[S]** — [ICAN formula](notes/this-2-minute-tell-me-about-yourself-magic-formula-gets.md)
- Omitting the "why this role, now" sentence makes you sound directionless. **[S]** — same
- Close by asking whether they'd like more detail on any part — turns a monologue into an exchange. **[S]** — same
- Rehearse this one specifically because it lands at peak nerves. **[S]** —
  [Under 10 Minutes](notes/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-under-10-minutes-last.md)
- Answer-length window, Emily Durham **[F]**: over 90 s–2 min the interviewer tunes out; under
  30 s it doesn't register as an answer at all.
  — [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)
- **Counter-example worth studying**: McMunn's **[S]** sample answer contains no employer, experience,
  or metric — a string of adjectives plus a promise of impact. Recorded in the corpus as the shape
  to avoid. — [Under 10 Minutes](notes/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-under-10-minutes-last.md)

## Stage 4 — Behavioral questions

### Structure

- Use a named framework (STAR, CAR, HERO). Sharghi's **[F]** argument for structure: hiring
  managers evaluate by pattern-matching, so an unstructured answer reads as confusion, not depth.
  — [The Interview Secret](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-the-interview-secret-to.md)
- **Google XYZ formula** as the default shape: _accomplished X as measured by Y by doing Z_.
  Her claim: anything you can't quantify won't get you hired. **[F]** — same
- STAR should read as **one continuous story, not four labelled blocks**. **[P]** —
  [Behavioral is shockingly uncomplicated](notes/answering-behavioral-interview-questions-is-shockingly.md)
- Durham **[F]** defends STAR against "it's dated": one to two sentences per letter naturally lands
  the answer inside the acceptable length window.
  — [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)

### Delivery is the differentiator

- Matt Huang **[P]**: the technical/case portion is roughly **10%** of the outcome for competitive
  roles. Given two candidates with identical facts, the one who narrates internal reactions and
  future-paces wins "9 times out of 10." (His figures, unmeasured.)
  — [The best interview advice you never got](notes/the-best-interview-advice-you-never-got-get-into-google-bcg.md)
- **Show don't tell**: "my boss told me I messed up and I felt upset" vs. "the first thought that
  came to my mind was _boy am I screwed_." Stated mechanism: you cannot filter your thoughts, only
  your speech, so voicing the unfiltered reaction reads as authentic. The raw thought is the setup —
  it only works if you immediately pivot to what you did with it. **[P]** — same
- **Future-pacing**: end by connecting the lesson to a concrete scenario in _this_ role. He frames
  it as manufacturing an "inception" effect — the interviewer constructs the image of you already
  working there without realizing they're being steered. **[P]** — same
- Close every story with "what I learned from that experience is X" — without it the interviewer
  has to infer the takeaway. **[P]** —
  [Behavioral is shockingly uncomplicated](notes/answering-behavioral-interview-questions-is-shockingly.md)
- Reality check: they're seeing 10–20 candidates a week and have already heard your business-club
  story from five other people this month. Attention is earned by the telling. **[P]** — same
- Replace filler words with silence — pauses feel far longer to you than to the interviewer. If you
  need to break eye contact, look sideways rather than down. **[P]** — same
- For high-hour jobs (~70–80 hrs/wk consulting/banking), Huang **[P]** claims the dominant filter is
  literally "can I picture being in the office with this person at 10pm on a Tuesday."
  — [The best interview advice](notes/the-best-interview-advice-you-never-got-get-into-google-bcg.md)

### The specific omissions candidates make

- **They skip _why it was hard_**, assuming it's self-evident. Fix with an explicit comparison:
  "this normally takes three weeks and we had five days" — the interviewer has never worked in
  your department and cannot infer difficulty. Cass Thompson **[S]** —
  [Top 10 Questions](notes/top-10-most-common-job-interview-questions-answered.md)
- **They state the result as their own action completing** ("so I talked to them and we finished it")
  rather than as perceived by other people — does the difficult client still hate you, how did the
  team react. That's what reads as resolution. **[S]** — same
- Teamwork answers should size and name the team (six people? twenty? which departments?) and state
  your specific role. **[S]** — same
- The under-used fourth element of a failure story: _"here's how I'm now helping teammates hitting
  the same thing."_ It converts a failure into evidence of impact. **[S]** —
  [8 Most-Asked Questions](notes/8-most-asked-interview-questions-answers-for-freshers.md)

### Question-specific traps

- **"Tell me about your proudest accomplishment" is a resilience question, not a bragging slot** —
  the interviewer cares about the hurdle you cleared to get from A to B, not the accomplishment.
  Most candidates answer the surface question. **[P]** —
  [Behavioral is shockingly uncomplicated](notes/answering-behavioral-interview-questions-is-shockingly.md)
- **Ethical-conflict questions are a two-sided trap**: tattletale on one end, ignores-misconduct on
  the other. Thread it by explicitly saying you didn't want the person to get in trouble. **[P]** — same
- **"Where do you see yourself in 5 years"** — Sharghi **[F]** states what's actually assessed:
  (1) are you leaving in six months, (2) will you get bored and become a problem. Saying "hopefully
  in a leadership role _somewhere_" reads as "not here"; grad school and starting your own business
  are disqualifying. — [Why "Lying" Gets You Hired](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-why-lying-gets-you-hired.md)
  Cass Thompson **[S]**: naming a title is fine, naming a date isn't.
- **"Why do you want this job"** — the interviewer is pricing training cost against attrition risk.
  A money-only motivation predicts you leave for the next offer. **[S]** —
  [8 Most-Asked Questions](notes/8-most-asked-interview-questions-answers-for-freshers.md)
- **Hobbies** — Sharghi's **[F]** firsthand contrast: a marketing candidate mentioned restoring a
  vintage motorcycle and the hiring manager raised it _three times in the debrief_. Signal read as
  curiosity, patience, willingness to figure things out. "Watching TV" gives them nothing to use.
  — [Why "Lying" Gets You Hired](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-why-lying-gets-you-hired.md)
- **Illegal questions** (marriage, children, gender, race) — one India-market source **[S]** treats
  them as a live risk that gets asked anyway and advises gracefully redirecting to the job rather
  than answering or objecting. Survival advice for an unenforced market, not an endorsement.
  — [8 Most-Asked Questions](notes/8-most-asked-interview-questions-answers-for-freshers.md)
  Sharghi **[F]** notes that in the US employers legally can't ask about family status and won't
  push on a "personal reasons" answer.

## Stage 5 — The dangerous questions

### Weakness

Both **[F]** sources treat this as adversarial by design. The Companies Expert **[F]** (taught
recruiting at postgraduate level): the recruiter's explicit job is to peel back the sales pitch and
extract disqualifying information, and recruiters get visibly uncomfortable when that's said aloud.
— [What Is Your Weakness](notes/what-is-your-weakness-best-answer-from-former-ceo-the.md)

- **The comparative-set argument** — the sharpest claim in the corpus on this question: your honesty
  isn't judged in isolation. If the other nine candidates volunteered nothing bad, the one who
  disclosed a real flaw is the only one with a known negative, and that alone loses the offer.
  Disclosure is penalized _relative to a field that didn't disclose_. **[F]**
- **The recruiter's verbal reward for candor ("I appreciate you shooting from the hip") is a tell,
  not a signal of success** — it precedes never calling back. **[F]**
- Recommended structure: an **understandable side effect of something good** — a real tradeoff —
  followed by the concrete habit that neutralizes it. "I'm not the fastest worker, but I verify
  everything I submit — and when I'm handed an assignment I confirm the timeline up front, so the
  extra time never surprises anyone." **The mitigation half is what scores**, not the weakness. **[F]**
- The tradeoff runs in either direction: "slower but most accurate" and "fast at 90% with high
  volume" both work — pick the one that's true of you. **[F]**
- Asked in disguise too ("what's something you're working on about yourself"), so prepare substance,
  not a script keyed to wording. **[F]**
- Cass Thompson **[S]** adds one constraint: the weakness must **not be adjacent to the job's core
  skill**. — [Top 10 Questions](notes/top-10-most-common-job-interview-questions-answered.md)
- **Avoid the stock three** — weak public speaking, no leadership experience yet, difficulty saying
  no. Offered as safe defaults by McMunn **[S]**, and flagged in that same note as exactly what a
  recruiter will recognize as scripted.
  — [Under 10 Minutes](notes/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-under-10-minutes-last.md)

### Never badmouth a previous employer

Three independent framings of _why_, and none of them is etiquette:

- **Prediction**: someone who disliked their last employer will find reasons to dislike this one.
  The complaint is read as a forecast about you. Don Georgevich **[S]** —
  [5 Dangerous Things](notes/5-dangerous-things-to-avoid-saying-in-a-job-interview.md)
- **Risk**: the recruiter's actual internal thought is _"what is she going to say about us in two
  years?"_ — and in a stack of qualified candidates, risks get cut. Sharghi **[F]** —
  [Why "Lying" Gets You Hired](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-why-lying-gets-you-hired.md)
- **Credibility**: the interviewer doesn't know you yet and has no basis to judge how much of your
  account to believe. Cass Thompson **[S]** —
  [Top 10 Questions](notes/top-10-most-common-job-interview-questions-answered.md)

Sharghi's **[F]** firsthand incident: a candidate described her current manager as a "micromanager…
too hands-on" and said she wanted more autonomy. The hiring manager in the room was internally known
for being heavily involved in her team's work. No offer, never told why. **You don't know who you're
talking to or who they know.**

On "why are you leaving," Cass Thompson **[S]** adds a delivery note: the real failure is
over-explaining out of anxiety. Answer briefly, then deliberately stay silent and let them move on.

### Resume gaps

- Sharghi **[F]**: _"that gap already got you the interview. We saw it. We still called you."_ The
  gap is priced in; the only remaining variable is your tone. Defensive or apologetic delivery
  creates the problem. Layoffs → say layoffs. Personal/health/caregiving → "I took time off for
  personal reasons and now I'm ready to return."
  — [Why "Lying" Gets You Hired](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-why-lying-gets-you-hired.md)
- John Lees via HBR **[S]**: prepare **two levels** for any uncomfortable resume fact — a short
  surface answer plus a deeper one held in reserve for the follow-up, so you're never improvising
  on your weakest fact. Worked example for an unfinished degree: first "I felt it was better to go
  straight into the workforce," then only if pushed, "I knew it would carry negative connotations,
  but I was confident I would learn more with direct work experience."
  — [HBR Guide](notes/stand-out-in-a-job-interview-the-harvard-business-review.md)

### Other instant-loss statements

- "I'll do anything" — the employer expects you unhappy in two or three months, i.e. a mis-hire they
  pay for twice. It's a cost calculation, not a judgment about enthusiasm. **[S]** —
  [5 Dangerous Things](notes/5-dangerous-things-to-avoid-saying-in-a-job-interview.md)
- Trait labels ("self-starter", "quick learner") — the label is something the interviewer assigns
  _after_ hearing the specifics. Claiming it yourself preempts the conclusion they were about to
  draw. Also: describe how you _have_ done things, not how you _would_. **[S]** — same
- "So what does your company do?" — announces zero preparation. **[S]** — same

## Stage 6 — Not knowing the answer

This is the one skill three sources cover independently, and they converge.

- **Answering fast is read as not caring, not as being smart** — a quick reply signals you didn't
  process what was said. Vinh Giang **[S]** —
  [How to Answer ANY Question](notes/how-to-answer-any-question-even-if-you-don-t-know-the-answer.md)
- **Two visually distinct pauses, read oppositely.** Positive: eyes to the side, hand raised, thinker
  pose — reads as processing. Negative: slouching, looking down, fidgeting — reads as anxiety and not
  knowing. Same silence, opposite interpretation, decided entirely by posture. **[S]** — same
- Pause 2–3 seconds; if that isn't enough, offer a scheduled follow-up ("would it be helpful if I
  met with you later today and gave you a comprehensive answer") rather than "I don't know." **[S]** — same
- **Answer through a framework, not free association.** Without one you "speak out your thought
  process" — narrating your brain's associative path — and the value is lost in the rambling
  regardless of content quality. **[S]** — same
- **The 3-2-1 framework**: three steps / two types / one thing. Pick one shape and speak only in it.
  Drill it by having someone throw a random topic with a **3-second countdown**. Giang's show of
  hands at his own workshop: under 10% of the room uses any communication framework at all
  (anecdotal, one room). **[S]** —
  [The 3-2-1 Speaking Trick](notes/the-3-2-1-speaking-trick-that-forces-you-to-stop-rambling.md)
- The rambling and the blank are **one mechanism, not two**: with no framework you either verbalize
  the search or the search stalls. **[S]** — same
- **The bridging move**: "I haven't done that exact thing, but I've done something similar." Showing
  _no_ recognition of the topic is the actual red flag. The "ask to circle back" lifeline works but
  is capped at once or twice. **[S]** —
  [5 Dangerous Things](notes/5-dangerous-things-to-avoid-saying-in-a-job-interview.md)
- Mid-interview recovery when you've visibly stumbled: _"Am I giving you what you need? Is there
  other information I could give you?"_ Then treat the next question as if it were the first. **[S]** —
  [HBR Guide](notes/stand-out-in-a-job-interview-the-harvard-business-review.md)
- Close an answer with "did you want me to go deeper on that?" — lets the asker pull for depth
  instead of you guessing. **[S]** —
  [How to Answer ANY Question](notes/how-to-answer-any-question-even-if-you-don-t-know-the-answer.md)

## Stage 7 — The technical / coding round

### The process

Anthony D. Mays **[P]** teaches six steps: restate the problem → clarifying questions → work
concrete examples → brainstorm 2–3 approaches → implement → test.
[6 Steps](notes/how-to-solve-any-coding-interview-question-in-6-steps.md)

- **Implementation should take the _least_ time of any step.** Starting to code immediately after
  hearing the problem is wrong "99% of the time." **[P]**
- **Don't pseudocode** — contra common advice. Many interviews require real, syntactically correct
  code, so anything written "for fake" gets written twice. Extended into a readiness bar: if you
  don't know where the curly braces go in your language, you aren't ready to interview. **[P]**
- **The interviewer already knows the best answer, the worst, and everything between** — they've run
  this question many times. Brainstorming out loud isn't about being right, it's **ammunition they
  can use to push you in a direction**. **[P]**
- Mechanical lever when stuck on a naive solution: the space-time tradeoff. Better time → add a data
  structure (hash map, graph, stack, queue, heap). Better space → drop the data structures and accept
  more loops. **[P]**
- Settle explicitly what "optimal" means here: fastest Big O, best space, or fastest to implement. **[P]**
- A clarifying question turned back on you ("why that method and not this one?") is a probe of your
  reasoning, not a refusal to answer. **[P]**
- Permission to stop optimizing: if only a naive solution comes, implement it and move on —
  sometimes you can't see the better one until the naive one is written. **[P]**

### What the interviewer is actually scoring

Robert Heaton **[F]** (400+ coding interviews over ten years) — the most concretely actionable
firsthand source in the corpus:
[How to pass a coding interview with me](notes/how-to-pass-a-coding-interview-with-me-robert-heaton.md)

- **Partial credit is the organising principle** behind nearly every tip. Talking, running code
  often, sketching first, and leaving `TODO`s all exist to give him something to award when the code
  doesn't fully work.
- **Don't abstract.** Leave the block inline with `TODO: probably extract this into a method`. His
  reasoning: you can't iterate in an interview, you're more likely to be wrong having thought about
  it for only minutes, _and_ good programmers disagree about correct abstractions — so an abstraction
  opens "a disagreement where you can only ever lose, even if you're right." Be quick to _note_ an
  abstraction, slow to implement it.
- **Don't use a testing framework** unless required or you're genuinely fluent — setup is easy to get
  wrong and eats time. Hand-rolled `if actual == expected: print("Pass")` is enough.
- **If all your tests pass first time, deliberately introduce a bug** and confirm some fail. Passing
  first time more likely means your test setup is broken than that you're a genius.
- **Print "Pass" on success, not just failure** — silent output is ambiguous between "worked" and
  "exited early for some erroneous reason."
- **Don't do TDD** to impress him; he finds it costs a lot of time for little interview benefit.
- Debug by stating explicit hypotheses out loud ("my hypothesis is the filter is wrong; I'll print
  the list after it and expect 5 elements") rather than poking.
- Ask how long you have and **how far you're expected to get** — some interviews deliberately contain
  more parts than anyone could finish. Ask whether they want efficient code, clean code, or both,
  and whether they want tests.
- Asking for help early isn't unrecoverable — he'll nudge you anyway once it's the only way forward,
  so saying it early buys back a minute, and he might forget it happened.
- Three trivial derailers: not saving the file, relative-path mistakes reading input (use the
  absolute path), and mutating vs. non-mutating methods (`list.sort()` returns `None`).
- Write `TODO`s for validation, error handling, and edge cases instead of doing them; sweep back
  after the base case works. Unaddressed `TODO`s still show you knew what was needed.
- On Googling questions beforehand: he has to ding you if he notices. Several interviewees left a tab
  with the leaked question open — all of them did quite poorly anyway.

### Getting stuck is expected

Keith Galli **[P]** in the freeCodeCamp mock interview: needing hints is the difference between a
_good_ interview and a _perfect_ one, not between pass and fail. Hard questions are chosen
deliberately to spread the pool; most of the strong half won't see the answer immediately, and what's
scored is the rebound. Kylie Ying's candidate-side reframe: an interview where you took a hint and
ran with it shows the interviewer they can unblock you.
— [Full Mock Interview](notes/software-engineering-job-interview-full-mock-interview.md)

- **Raising testing yourself is a scored signal.** Salvage line when time runs out: name the edge
  cases you _would_ have tested. **[P]**
- Galli's OOD criticisms were about **decomposition, not correctness** — a separate `Display` class
  to isolate presentation from data; subclassing (`Book` / `UserBook`) for the multi-user follow-up. **[P]**
- Why the loop had both an OOD and an algorithms half: a single algorithm question doesn't tell him
  whether you'd be a productive team member, and OOD alone doesn't either. **[P]**
- Most-repeated positive: **narrate while you type** — it's the only way he can tell whether you're
  on track. Echoed by Heaton **[F]**: silence still passes if the code is perfect, but commentary is
  insurance for partial credit.
- The post-interview recovery email: a Google data-analyst candidate fumbled some SQL, then emailed
  corrected solutions afterward. Situational, but unlikely to hurt — short and casual. **[P]**

### Senior-level technical screens

From an unnamed interviewer's article relayed by Travis Media **[S]** (treat as secondhand):
design patterns function as a near-universal filter — he says he could copy-paste "read more about
design patterns" as feedback nearly every time. A real probe: _why does Python use `len(array)`
rather than `array.length`_ — the point isn't the answer, it's whether you've gone deep enough on
your language to have one. And **nodding along is a negative signal**: he wants "capacity of
criticism," including disagreement with his own stated strong opinions. Agreeing to be agreeable is
the failure mode being tested.
— [5 Coding Interview Tips](notes/5-coding-interview-tips-from-a-senior-engineer.md)

## Stage 8 — Rapport, and who is actually in the room

### The recruiter screen is not a technical evaluation

- Erin McGoff **[S]**: the first-round interviewer is usually HR/recruiting, **won't understand your
  domain jargon**, and is ticking boxes that are mostly keywords lifted from the job description.
  If the posting says Premiere Pro and Avid, say Premiere Pro and Avid.
  — [6 Steps](notes/6-steps-to-ace-your-job-interview-pep-talk.md)
- Emily Durham **[F]**: _"Recruiters are not experts in what you do. They're experts in recruiting."_
  Consequence: a small technical error often goes unnoticed, while an unconfident delivery never does.
  **Delivery outranks accuracy in the screen specifically because of who is scoring it.**
  — [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)
- Leila Hormozi **[F]**: vocabulary matching is an explicit screening signal from the employer's side —
  she listens for whether a sales candidate says "high ticket," says "closing." Drop industry-internal
  shorthand from your old sector; source the terminology from the job description.
  — [Watch this BEFORE](notes/watch-this-before-your-job-interview.md)
- The recruiter is structurally on your side: a hire means one less req to fill, or a commission. **[F]** —
  [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)
- Erin McGoff **[S]**: their job is to find someone hirable, so they're looking for reasons to say
  yes; a good candidate makes them look good to their own boss.

### The alike-lever

Andrew Bustamante **[S]** (ex-CIA): interviewers don't hire people they _like_, they hire people who
are _alike_. Seeing elements of themselves in you short-circuits sense-making straight to like-and-trust.
— [The Ultimate Hack](notes/the-ultimate-hack-to-nail-every-job-interview.md)

- Concrete tactic: **ask more questions than the interviewer does**, weighted open-ended, including
  while answering theirs. Explicitly not rapid-fire peppering.
- **Most interviewers are not the person who'd supervise the hire** — they're an intermediary, so
  their actual goal is narrow: confirm technical qualification and find common ground with themselves.
  This is why the lever works.
- The advantage comes from **scarcity, not cleverness** — nobody else that day will ask them anything.
- Steven Bartlett's firsthand counter-example in the same clip: an hour-long interview where the
  candidate talked the whole time and asked zero questions. He rejected them, rationalizing it as
  "that person would be difficult to deal with," and only recognizes mid-clip that the real driver
  was not being asked anything.
- His stated figure — this takes you to "a solid predictable 30% chance" — has no basis given.

Adjacent: Hormozi **[F]** on showing interest in the interviewer (ask how they like their role, why
they founded the company) and on compliments working even when visibly strategic (attributed to
unnamed studies — unverified). Vinh Giang **[S]** on the four-color model as a way to adapt delivery
to the person across from you, with a three-question silent diagnostic — noting that **the corpus
flags his question 2 as internally inconsistent with his own descriptions**; the mapping that matches
DISC is logical → blue or red, emotional → green or yellow.
— [Watch this BEFORE](notes/watch-this-before-your-job-interview.md),
[How to Talk to ANYONE](notes/how-to-talk-to-anyone-once-you-know-their-color.md)

## Stage 9 — Your questions at the end

Treated by the corpus as an intelligence-gathering slot, not a politeness ritual.

**Ask:**

- _What would make someone exceed expectations in the first six months? What measurable outcomes
  define success?_ — the answers tell you what you'd actually be graded on. Sharghi **[F]** —
  [The Interview Secret](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-the-interview-secret-to.md)
- _Why is this position open?_ — your diagnostic: promoted, quit, or fired. Sharghi **[F]** —
  [Why "Lying" Gets You Hired](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-why-lying-gets-you-hired.md)
- One question about the **recruiter personally** (tenure, what drew them to the company). Durham **[F]**
  says this happens so rarely that recruiters remember it, and it proves you looked at their LinkedIn.
  — [Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)
- What should I concentrate on first / in the first 30 days; what does success look like 12 months
  out; tell me about the team. McMunn **[S]** —
  [Under 10 Minutes](notes/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-under-10-minutes-last.md)
- Something current and specific from your research ("I saw your competitor released a sustainable
  product line — will you be following suit?"). **[S]** —
  [6 Steps](notes/6-steps-to-ace-your-job-interview-pep-talk.md)
- Open-ended questions that follow from what you just heard, so the exchange unfolds. **[S]** —
  [HBR Guide](notes/stand-out-in-a-job-interview-the-harvard-business-review.md)

**Don't ask:**

- **About company culture** — Sharghi **[F]**: you'll get a canned answer and learn nothing.
  (Note: Cass Thompson **[S]** recommends culture/day-to-day questions. Weight the **[F]** source.)
- Pay, vacation, holiday, remote flexibility in a first interview — that's a negotiation
  conversation. **[F]** and **[S]**, unanimous across the corpus.

**The close.** Sharghi's **[F]** "end like a consultant" script: name the problem and goal you heard,
say what you'd focus on if you started next week, then ask _"does that align with what you're
envisioning for this role?"_ It converts the ending from waiting for approval into checking alignment.
— [The Interview Secret](notes/ex-google-recruiter-explains-the-interview-secret-to.md)

McMunn's **[S]** more unusual variant: bring a written **30/60/90-day action plan** and physically
hand it over when they ask "why should we hire you."
— [Under 10 Minutes](notes/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-under-10-minutes-last.md)

## Stage 10 — Salary and follow-up

Only one source in the corpus covers salary — Emily Durham **[F]**:
[Interview Secrets](notes/interview-secrets-that-make-you-the-top-candidate.md)

- Deflect first: _"do you happen to have a budget for the role just so I know if we're aligned?"_
  Only give a number if pushed.
- Her worked example inflates the _baseline_ only slightly — earning $50k, market average $60k, she
  says state $55k, leaving room to raise it later. **The corpus flags this: as stated it anchors
  below market.** She's optimizing for negotiating room, not the opening number. Judge for yourself.
- If asked whether you're interviewing elsewhere, **say yes — explicitly including if you're lying**.
  Phrase it as "I'm in a couple of late-stage conversations," never naming companies. Rationale is
  demand signaling.
- Thank-you note referencing something specific from the conversation. Its second function: if you
  haven't heard back in a week, **reply-all on that thread** — a less awkward vehicle for the
  follow-up than a fresh message.
